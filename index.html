SOURCE 1

A Classroom Without Walls

by Maya Bennett

1At Pine Hill Elementary, one classroom has no desks, walls, or ceiling. It is the school garden. Students grow lettuce, tomatoes, herbs, and flowers in raised beds. Teachers use the garden for lessons throughout the year.

2A garden can make science easier to understand. Instead of only reading about plant parts, students can examine real roots, stems, leaves, and flowers. They can observe insects, measure rainfall, and record how quickly plants grow. These activities give students evidence they can see and touch.

3Gardens can support other subjects too. Students use multiplication to plan equal rows of seeds. They calculate the area of planting beds and write observations in journals. When vegetables are ready, students can study nutrition or use the harvest in a simple recipe.

A school garden can become an outdoor classroom.

4Time outdoors may also help students return to class ready to focus. Digging, watering, and pulling weeds give students a chance to move. Working in teams requires students to share tools, solve problems, and care for something together.

5A school does not need a large field to begin. A few containers in a sunny area can hold herbs or small vegetables. Families and local businesses may donate seeds, soil, or tools. Starting small allows a school to learn what works before making the garden larger.

What Students Can Practice in a Garden

Observing changes • Measuring length and area • Collecting data • Writing explanations • Working as a team
SOURCE 2

Before We Plant

by Jordan Lee

1A school garden sounds exciting, but it needs more than seeds and sunshine. Before planting, a school must decide who will do the work, how much the garden will cost, and what will happen when school is closed.

2Plants require steady care. During hot weather, some vegetables need water every day. Students may be away on weekends, holidays, and summer break. Without a watering plan, plants can dry out quickly. Weeds and insects may also damage a garden if no one checks it regularly.

3Costs can add up. A garden may need soil, lumber or containers, gloves, tools, seeds, and a hose. Old boards can splinter, and tools must be stored safely. Schools must also choose a location with enough sunlight that does not block playgrounds or walkways.

A Sample Small-Garden Budget

Containers: $45
Soil and compost: $60
Seeds and plants: $35
Tools and gloves: $55
Total: $195

4Teachers already have many lessons to prepare. If garden jobs take too much instructional time, the project may become a burden. A garden works best when it connects directly to learning instead of becoming one more chore.

5These challenges do not mean a school should never plant a garden. They mean the school needs a realistic plan. A garden committee could create a schedule, set a budget, and recruit family volunteers. Beginning with two or three containers would let students test the idea before the school spends more money.

6Careful planning is as important as planting. If a school cannot provide time, money, and regular care, it should choose another outdoor project. If the school can solve those problems, a small garden may grow into a useful learning space.

Consider what you have learned about school gardens from reviewing the two sources. You will now construct an original text using your own ideas. In your text, answer the research question for the specific purpose and audience shown below.

Research Question: Should our school create an outdoor learning garden?

Purpose: to provide and support your opinion about whether the school should create an outdoor learning garden

Audience: your principal, teachers, and classmates

Be sure to give reasons for your opinion and support those reasons with information from both sources. Refer to the Writer’s Checklist as you plan, write, and edit your text.

Grade 4-5 Writer's Checklist

In my well-written text, I made sure to:

  • introduce the topic clearly.
  • state my opinion or position.
  • organize reasons and evidence in a logical order.
  • support each reason with evidence from both sources.
  • explain how the evidence supports my reason.
  • use linking words and phrases to connect ideas.
  • use precise words suited to my audience.
  • provide a conclusion related to the opinion presented.
  • correct errors in spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and grammar.
